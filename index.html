About

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the University of British Columbia. My research interests are theoretical and applied econometrics. My current work focuses on the econometrics of discrete choice models with costly information acquisition.

My job market paper asks whether choice data can distinguish unobserved payoff heterogeneity from costly information acquisition. In a rational inattention discrete choice model, I establish conditions for semiparametric identification and develop estimation and inference methods using aggregate choice data.

I expect to graduate in 2027 and am on the 2026–2027 academic job market.

Research

Job Market Paper

Estimating a Rational Inattention Discrete Choice Model: A Semiparametric Approach

People’s choices depend on both the payoffs of the available options and what they learn about those payoffs before deciding. This paper develops methods to distinguish unobserved payoff heterogeneity from costly information acquisition in a rational inattention discrete choice model using aggregate choice data. Under Shannon information costs, I establish conditions for semiparametric point identification using exogenous utility and information-cost shifters, leaving the taste shock distribution and the information-cost function nonparametric. The identification argument begins by interpreting each binary choice curve, as a function of an appropriately oriented utility shifter, as a cumulative distribution function. I construct an auxiliary random variable with this distribution and show how its moments identify payoff coefficients and the information-cost function. Environments in which information costs approach zero provide the full-information benchmark needed for this recovery. A standard deconvolution argument then identifies the taste shock distribution. I extend identification to multiple alternatives and establish consistency of a sieve minimum-distance estimator. Because parameter constraints can place estimates on the boundary and invalidate conventional normal approximations, I construct confidence regions by test inversion without requiring asymptotic normality of the estimator. An application to Amazon Mechanical Turk illustrates the implications of information costs for workers’ responses to wages.

Presented at the Econometric Society North American Summer Meeting (Emory University, 2026) and the Canadian Economics Association Annual Conference (Simon Fraser University, 2026).

Work in Progress

Directed Search with Rational Inattention

This paper studies directed search when workers must acquire costly information about wages before deciding where to apply. Workers observe firm types but not realized wages and acquire information at a Shannon entropy cost. We define a rationally inattentive search equilibrium in which application choices determine congestion across submarkets, while congestion shapes job-finding probabilities and the returns to information. We first establish equilibrium existence when the wage distribution has finite support and job-finding probabilities depend continuously on workers’ application probabilities. The existence proof allows workers to have multiple optimal information-acquisition strategies. It exploits the fact that linear-fractional maps preserve convexity, together with workers’ optimality conditions, to apply Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem. We then establish closed-form identification of worker-type-specific information costs using twin job types with identical amenity values but different wages. Given known matching technology, conditional and ex-ante choice shares identify information costs when the twins offer equal job-finding probabilities or their common amenity value is zero, subject to positive shares and a nonzero identifying contrast. Identification relates the twins’ difference in expected wage income to the difference between their conditional and ex-ante log choice odds. Together, these results provide an equilibrium foundation and an empirical strategy for measuring attention costs in job search.
