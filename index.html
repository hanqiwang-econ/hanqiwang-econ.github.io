About

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the University of British Columbia. My research interests are theoretical and applied econometrics. My current work focuses on the econometrics of discrete choice models with costly information acquisition.

My job market paper studies choices made by individuals who are initially unsure about their payoffs and can learn about them at a cost. I ask whether choice data can identify both unobserved payoff heterogeneity and the cost of learning. I establish conditions for semiparametric identification of discrete choice under rational inattention and develop estimation and inference methods using aggregate choice data.

I expect to graduate in 2027 and am on the 2026–2027 job market.

Fields: Econometric Theory; Applied Econometrics; Microeconomic Theory

Research

Job Market Paper

Estimating a Rational Inattention Discrete Choice Model: A Semiparametric Approach

Presented at the Econometric Society North American Summer Meeting (Emory University, 2026) and the Canadian Economics Association Annual Conference (Simon Fraser University, 2026).

Abstract|Paper

People’s choices depend on both the payoffs of the available options and what they learn about those payoffs before deciding. This paper shows how choice data identify both unobserved payoff heterogeneity and the cost of information acquisition in a rational inattention discrete-choice model. I establish conditions for semiparametric point identification using exogenous payoff and information-cost shifters, without imposing parametric forms on the taste-shock distribution or on how the unit cost of information varies with observed characteristics. In the model with binary choice, I construct an unobserved choice threshold whose cumulative distribution function is the choice probability as a function of the payoff shifter. Moments of this threshold identify the payoff coefficients and the unit cost of information at each value of the information-cost shifter. A deconvolution argument then identifies the taste-shock distribution. I then extend identification to the model with multinomial choice. To estimate the model, I propose a sieve minimum-distance estimator based on observed market shares and establish its consistency. For inference, I construct confidence regions by test inversion because binding parameter constraints can make the estimator’s limiting distribution nonnormal. In an application to Amazon Mechanical Turk, model counterfactuals show that lower unit costs of information raise the average task-taking probability and reduce wage elasticity.

Work in Progress

Directed Search with Rational Inattention

Abstract|Paper

This paper develops a directed search model for estimating workers’ job preferences and information costs from application data. Workers observe firm types but not realized wages and choose what wage information to acquire before deciding where to apply. Information costs take the Shannon entropy form and can vary across worker types. I define a rationally inattentive search equilibrium in which application choices determine congestion across submarkets, while congestion shapes job-finding probabilities and the returns to information. I establish equilibrium existence when the wage distribution has finite support and job-finding probabilities depend continuously on workers’ application probabilities. The proof’s key insight is to use the linear-fractional structure of the generalized logit formula to establish existence without requiring a unique solution to workers’ information-acquisition problems. I also establish conditions for identifying worker-type-specific information costs using twin job types with identical amenity values but different wages.

Work in Progress

Identification of a Targeted Search Model for Two-sided Matching Markets

Abstract|Paper

Matching among similar people may reflect preferences or the composition of their social circles. I establish conditions for identifying preference and social-circle parameters from observed search behavior. Building on Cheremukhin et al. (2020, 2015), I develop a two-sided search model with unobserved payoff heterogeneity, allowing the value of a match to vary across individuals. Individuals choose how likely they are to contact each partner type, such as an education group. Social circles determine the probabilities of contacting each type without additional effort. A targeting cost captures the effort required to redirect search. Matches require reciprocal search. I establish equilibrium existence and show that social circles can generate matching among similar partners when targeting costs are high, even without a preference for similarity. Individual contact probabilities identify targeting costs through quantile comparisons that use a specified distribution of unobserved payoff heterogeneity. Observed social circles then allow recovery of preferences. When circles are unobserved, I give conditions under which changes in targeting costs identify both preferences and social-circle composition. When only average contact probabilities are observed for each type, I compare environments with different social circles but common preferences and targeting costs, and establish conditions for local identification of preferences and targeting costs.
